He was a troubled kid. That is the plain version, and he does not dress it up. What he could not explain to anyone at the time was that things kept happening around him — things he felt before they arrived, things he knew without being told. He had no framework for any of it. No elder, no house, no language. So it frightened him, and he did what frightened people do with a gift they cannot name: he pushed it away and got on with his life.

Spirituality did not accept the dismissal. It followed him. Through the years he spent building something else, through the work, through the ordinary business of getting older, it stayed at the edge of the room and waited.

The something else was real estate. He learned how property is valued and how it is financed. He learned what a credit file does to a person's options, how a deal is structured, where the margin sits and who is taking it. He built a working knowledge of the material world that most people never acquire — and he built it before he had any spiritual training at all.

Then he came near death. And when there was nothing else on the table, the answer that came back was Ifá — not as an interest, but as the road, and the priesthood as the walk.

He took it. That was eighteen years ago. Since then he has served as a babaláwo, and the sign he received at itá — Ogundá Masa — is the one this house is named for.

What that history produced is unusual. Most people who can read for you cannot read a settlement statement. Most people who can read a settlement statement have nothing to say about your orí. Ramon spent years in each world, in that order, and can move between them without confusing the two — which matters, because they are not the same thing and must never be sold as if they were.

He can also take someone the whole distance. From a first reading, through the receiving of elekes and the warriors, to Orula, and — where Ifá indicates it and the person is truly ready — through initiation into the priesthood itself. Not everyone is meant for that road. He will tell you honestly which part of it is yours.

How the house works

One reading. Then the right person for each thing it surfaces.

Nothing is diagnosed at you and nothing is sold in the room. The reading tells you what is in front of you. Where part of it is material rather than spiritual, you are introduced to the associate who handles that, and you are free to decline without anything changing.

1 · The reading

Ifá is consulted through the opèlè or the ikin. An odù speaks, and it is read for your situation — not a general fortune, but your particular road, with what supports it and what obstructs it.

2 · What it surfaces

Some of it is spiritual and is answered spiritually — ẹbọ, cleansing, ancestral work, a step on the road. Some of it is not. Debt is not a spiritual condition. Neither is an underwritten property or a company without books.

3 · The right hands

What is material goes to the associate who does that work — in writing, with no pressure and no condition attached. Your standing in this house does not depend on whether you take the introduction.

Membership

A house you belong to, not a service you buy once

Most people meet a reader once in a hard month, act on it or don't, and are never seen again. Membership exists because that is the least useful way to use this tradition — and because guidance only compounds when it is continuous.

The twenty-one day cadence is the centre of it. Three weeks is long enough for something to have actually happened, and short enough that a road can still be corrected before it hardens. It is the rhythm the house keeps.

Every member sitting is recorded in writing. Over a year that record becomes something no single reading can give you: a document of what was said, what you did about it, and what followed. Members read their own year back and see the pattern themselves.

Membership also changes how the house holds you. Members are known. When something breaks at an awkward hour, you are not a stranger writing to an inbox — and when a material issue surfaces, the introduction to the right associate is already warm.

What a membership year looks like

  1. Day one — an opening registro and the year's direction set down in writing.
  2. Every 21 days — a member sitting. Roughly seventeen across the year.
  3. Between sittings — the ẹbọ or observances indicated, and someone to ask when you are unsure.
  4. Monthly — community observance at the house. Open to members and their families.
  5. At the year's turn — a reading for the year ahead, and the whole record bound and given to you.
